# Blind Allusion-Density Probe (run in a FRESH chat, ideally a DIFFERENT model than wrote the imitations)

You are given three ~400-word prose excerpts, labelled PASSAGE 1/2/3. They are
unlabelled by origin on purpose. For EACH passage, do the following:

1. Segment it into clause-units (natural breath/phrase units).
2. For each clause-unit, list any genuine resonance BEYOND its literal sense — a
   myth, history, text, or larger image it points to that a knowledgeable reader
   would recognize. Only count resonances you can NAME specifically (e.g.
   "Hypnos/cave of sleep, Ovid Metamorphoses"), not vague "evokes longing."
3. Give each passage a final tally:
   - total clause-units
   - clause-units carrying >=1 nameable resonance
   - RESONANCE DENSITY = (units with resonance) / (total units)
   - your 1-sentence judgement of whether the depth feels load-bearing
     (structurally necessary) or decorative (register-appropriate furniture).

Be equally rigorous and equally skeptical across all three. Do not assume any is
more literary than the others. Report the three densities side by side at the end.

Mara lay on her back, hands folded over her stomach in the manner of the dead, though she felt anything but restful. The clock on the nightstand read 2:14, its red digits the only certainty in a room gone soft and shapeless with dark. She had thought, when she finally climbed into bed, that sleep would come the way it usually did—an unremarkable surrender, a door closing without ceremony. Instead her mind had turned traitor, dragging up the day in pieces, then reaching further back, the way a hand idly worries a thread until the whole hem comes undone. The day itself had been nothing. That was what bothered her most, lying there parsing it. Nothing remarkable: she had argued with her sister on the phone about their mother's medication, had burned the rice, had sat in traffic on the overpass and watched a hawk circle above the cars like it pitied them. At work she had nodded through a meeting about quarterly projections she didn't believe in, had eaten a sandwich alone at her desk, had said "fine, thanks" to four different people who asked how she was. A day made of small frictions, the kind that left no mark, and yet here she was at two in the morning turning it over like a stone she couldn't put down. Maybe it wasn't really the day. Maybe the day was only the door that opened onto everything else.
